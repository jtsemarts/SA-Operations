Data-Centric Architecture

Make your data the foundation that outlives every application.

One shared, meaning-rich model — so integration stops being a permanent cost and your AI has something solid to stand on.

What is data-centric architecture?Data-centric architecture makes data — not applications — the center of the enterprise. Instead of every system owning its own copy and interpretation of core concepts, all systems share one meaning-rich model (an ontology and knowledge graph) that outlives any application. It's the foundation that turns integration from a permanent cost into a solved problem — and makes enterprise AI reliable.
Why it matters

The application-centric tax

1Silos by default

Every application owns and defines its own data, so the same concept means ten different things across the estate.

2Integration debt

Ten systems don't mean ten models — they mean a tangle of brittle, point-to-point mappings tied to legacy code.

3AI on sand

Fragmented, contradictory data is exactly what stalls enterprise AI in pilot purgatory.

How SA does it

One model, built from your business

We model your organization's actual concepts — not its tables — into a compact enterprise ontology. Data-centric is the approach; a semantic substrate is what it produces — the ontology-grounded foundation every application, analysis, and AI draws from directly. New systems conform to the shared model automatically; existing ones integrate through its lens rather than point-to-point. It's a fixed-scope, gist-based engagement with no lock-in.

Knowledge Graphs & Ontologies

One coherent picture of your enterprise — queryable by people, systems, and AI.

One shared model of what your business means — the foundation every system, analysis, and AI draws from, not a layer bolted on top.

What is an enterprise knowledge graph?An enterprise knowledge graph is your organization's data connected according to a shared ontology — a model of what your business means, not just where its data sits. It's the shared foundation — a semantic substrate — that lets systems, analytics, and AI query one coherent picture instead of stitching together dozens of incompatible ones. Ontologies supply the meaning; the knowledge graph is where that meaning becomes queryable at enterprise scale.
Clear the vocabulary

Ontology, knowledge graph, semantic layer, semantic substrate

Ontology

The shared model of concepts and relationships — the meaning.

Knowledge graph

Your actual data, connected according to that model, at scale.

Semantic layer

A query-time band on top that translates business metrics for BI and AI, per tool — useful, but it depends on the systems beneath it.

Semantic substrate

The foundation underneath — one formally-defined model of meaning every application, analysis, and AI draws from directly.

What is a semantic substrate?A semantic substrate is the formally-defined foundation an enterprise's data is expressed in — one ontology-grounded model of meaning (RDF/OWL/SHACL) that every application, analysis, and AI system draws from directly. Where a semantic layer sits on top and translates business metrics at query time, per tool, a substrate sits underneath and defines meaning once, for everything — and is independent of the legacy systems, so it can absorb them over time instead of depending on them.

A semantic layer is a band on top; a semantic substrate is the ground underneath.

"A semantic layer translates meaning at the surface, one tool at a time. A semantic substrate builds meaning into the foundation — once, for everything."

Questions

FAQ

What's the difference between an ontology and a taxonomy?

A taxonomy classifies things into a hierarchy. An ontology also captures the relationships and rules between them — which is what makes it machine-reasonable and AI-usable.

Semantic layer vs. semantic substrate — what's the difference?

A layer sits on top and translates business metrics for BI/AI at query time, per tool, and depends on the systems beneath it. A substrate sits underneath as one formally-defined model of meaning that every consumer draws from directly, and is independent of those systems.

Is a semantic layer enough for enterprise AI?

It helps with metric consistency, which improves natural-language-to-SQL accuracy. It does not resolve the thousands of conflicting meanings underneath, which is what determines whether AI is trustworthy. For that you need meaning at the foundation — a semantic substrate.

Graph Database Alternative

RDF vs. property graph: which one survives contact with the enterprise?

Both store graphs. Only one is built for shared, portable, AI-ready meaning across your whole organization.

RDF/OWL or labeled property graph?For an enterprise knowledge graph, the choice is between the open RDF/OWL standard and a vendor's labeled-property-graph database. RDF wins for the enterprise because it's a W3C standard with formal semantics, portable meaning, and no vendor lock-in — the properties that matter when data must outlive tools and feed AI. Labeled property graphs optimize for single-application convenience; RDF optimizes for enterprise-wide, durable, AI-ready meaning.
The real question

It isn't traversal speed

Portability

RDF meaning moves between tools and vendors. LPG meaning is tied to one product.

Standards & validation

OWL and SHACL give you formal semantics and validation an application-scoped model can't.

No lock-in

The enterprise choice optimizes for durable meaning, not a single application's convenience.

You don't need another platform

Don't add a layer. Build the foundation.

The market keeps adding strata — a semantic layer, then a context layer, then a governance layer — each one a patch over the fact that meaning was never in the data. Put meaning at the foundation, and the layers you were about to buy become things the substrate already does. A context graph is a knowledge graph that still needs a formal ontology; a semantic substrate is that ontology-grounded foundation — the thing the whole tower is missing.

A layer sits on top and translates; a substrate sits underneath as the shared foundation.

Data-centric vs. data-driven: what's the difference?

DM
Dave McComb · Founder & President · 7 min read
TL;DRData-driven means using data to inform decisions. Data-centric means treating data as a durable asset that outlives every application — one shared model that all systems conform to. Most enterprises run data-driven analytics on top of an application-centric mess. Going data-centric is what makes both the data and the AI on top of it actually work.

Data-driven is about decisions. Data-centric is about architecture.

The two terms get used interchangeably, but they answer different questions. "Data-driven" is a decision-making posture: let the numbers, not opinions, guide what you do. "Data-centric" is an architectural stance: the data model is the center of gravity, and applications are replaceable things that plug into it — not the other way around.

You can be data-driven and still application-centric. In fact, most organizations are. Every dashboard and model is stitched together from data trapped inside dozens of applications, each with its own private, incompatible view of the same core concepts.

The application-centric default

When applications own the data, every new system multiplies the integration problem. Ten systems don't mean ten models — they mean a tangle of point-to-point mappings, each tied to legacy code. Data-centric architecture inverts that: one shared model, and systems conform to it.

Why the distinction matters for AI

AI is the forcing function. A model is only as good as the data it's grounded in, and fragmented, application-locked data is exactly what stalls enterprise AI in pilot purgatory. The organizations whose AI initiatives land are the ones that already treat data as a shared, meaning-rich asset.

AI is the door; data-centric is the house. Your hardest problems have always been data problems — now they're your AI problems too.

Being data-driven tells you to trust the data. Being data-centric is the work that makes the data trustworthy in the first place — and makes every AI project after it cheaper, faster, and more reliable.

Why AI pilots fail — and what actually fixes it

SA
Semantic Arts · 9 min read
TL;DRMost enterprise AI pilots fail for the same reason: the model is fine, but the data underneath is fragmented, ambiguous, and missing the business context an AI needs to reason. Retrieval-augmented and agentic systems inherit every inconsistency in the underlying data. Grounding AI on a knowledge graph — data connected by a shared ontology — is what turns a promising demo into a system you can trust in production.

The pilot that demos well and dies in production

The pattern is familiar: a proof-of-concept dazzles, then stalls the moment it meets real enterprise data. The instinct is to blame the model or the prompt. Usually the real cause is underneath — the data the system has to reason over doesn't share meaning.

What "AI-ready" actually means

AI-ready data is unified, unambiguous, and grounded: the same concept means the same thing everywhere, and the relationships between concepts are explicit enough for a machine to follow. That's not a data-cleaning task. It's a modeling task — the work of building an ontology and a knowledge graph.

Grounding, GraphRAG, and guardrails

When retrieval is grounded in a knowledge graph rather than loose text chunks, an AI can answer questions that depend on relationships across systems — and you can trace why it answered the way it did. That traceability is what makes AI defensible in a regulated enterprise.

The organizations whose AI lands are the ones that already treat data as a shared, meaning-rich asset.
